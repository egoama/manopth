# Scientific إعجاز: inventory and new-candidate search

## 1. Method
1. I went through the verses that mention natural phenomena: sky, earth, water, wind, mountains, stars, embryo, animals, plants and metals. I compared each one with the published i'jaz literature: Bucaille, Keith Moore, Zaghloul El-Naggar, Abdel-Daem Al-Kaheel, the هيئة الإعجاز العلمي, and online collections.
2. For readings that seemed under-discussed, I ran a quick web search to see whether they were already published. A search that finds nothing does not prove nobody has made the claim.
3. Each candidate had to meet all four criteria below. Failing any one means the verse cannot be a genuine miracle-type prediction.
   - **A. Clear wording:** the scientific meaning is the plain sense of the words, not a newer secondary sense.
   - **B. Not observable in the 7th century:** nobody could know it by observation or craft knowledge at the time.
   - **C. Specific:** the verse doesn't also fit the opposite scientific finding.
   - **D. Matches classical tafsir:** the commentators who wrote before the discovery read it the same way.

## 4. Conclusion
- The scientific-miracle literature has already gone through essentially every verse that mentions nature.
- What's left are readings that are either observable common knowledge or a strained fit to the words.
- I could easily write more "new miracles" of the stretched kind. They would come from the same method that produced the numerical coincidences in REPORT.md: choose a modern fact first, then pick the meaning of the word that fits it. I don't consider that a discovery.
- The well-supported tradition places إعجاز in the Quran's language and composition (نظم and بلاغة). Classical scholars such as al-Khattabi, al-Baqillani and al-Jurjani did the same. Scientific concordism is a 19th–20th century approach, and Islamic scholars such as al-Shatibi (in principle), Mahmud Shaltut and Amin al-Khuli criticised it.
